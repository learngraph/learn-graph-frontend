# LearnGraph Website Rulebook

The website is a graph, not a stack of pages.

Visitors move through:

`Territory → Topic → Content`

Every click is optional. Every click therefore has to earn the next one.

## 1. The Door Test

Before adding a node, ask:

**After reading this, is the visitor more likely to open another node or leave the website?**

If leaving is more likely, the node should not exist.

A weak node damages more than itself. It teaches the visitor that clicking may lead to hollow content.

Every published node therefore makes a promise about every unopened node.

Do not add nodes because:

- the graph looks more complete with them
- a feature technically exists
- there is empty space to fill
- the topic sounds marketable
- the copy can be made to sound important

**Usually it is better to prune than to add.**

A smaller graph with strong destinations is better than a complete-looking graph with weak ones.

## 2. A feature is not automatically a node

A button, upload, certificate, role, setting or workflow can be useful without carrying enough substance for its own destination.

A node needs a distinct idea, mechanism, consequence, tension or body of evidence.

If a small feature only becomes interesting after several paragraphs of explanation, cut the node.

**Never manufacture significance around thin functionality.**

## 3. Truth outranks topology

A node does not become true because the architecture needs it.

Code, partial workflow, planned capability and real end-to-end behaviour are different things. Do not present direction as current reality.

A reserved node may remain unpublished indefinitely.

## 4. The medium follows the idea

A node is not automatically an article.

Its strongest form may be one sentence, a diagram, an interface fragment, a case, a technical artifact, a video, or nothing yet.

Do not write prose because the display has room for it.

## 5. Write from substance

Start with what actually happens, what is unusual, what changes, what becomes possible, or what is worth understanding.

Then write.

Do not begin with a marketing claim and build a wrapper around it.

If the concrete thing is stronger than the explanation, use the concrete thing.

**Do not explain that something is interesting. Make the thing itself interesting.**

If something needs a wrapper to shine, it may simply not be strong enough to hold its ground.

## 6. One dominant thought

Every node needs one clear reason to exist.

It does not need to explain an entire product area.

Adjacent nodes must not repeat the same idea with different wording or at different speed.

If two nodes keep drawing from the same material, the split is probably wrong.

## 7. Compact by default

Length is earned by substance.

If removing a paragraph makes the node stronger, remove it.

If one sentence carries the idea, do not turn it into four.

Space is not something that needs to be filled.

## 8. Every element needs a job

Every paragraph must increase understanding or voltage.

Every visual must either:

**explain, prove, or intensify.**

Every interaction must teach, orient, connect or demonstrate.

If an element does none of these things, remove it.

## 9. Tone

Write like people who understand the thing they built.

Avoid:

- corporate marketing language
- pitch-deck cadence
- startup slogans
- generic empowerment copy
- trend words used as decoration
- inspirational endings added by habit
- em dashes used as automatic dramatic rhythm

Prefer things with weight:

- a mechanism someone can actually understand
- an observation specific enough to be remembered
- a real example
- a concrete consequence
- technical detail when the detail itself is interesting
- language that would stop making sense if LearnGraph were replaced by another product name

Precision carries more weight than importance-signalling.

## Before publishing

Ask whether the node has a reason to exist, whether every part of it earns its space, and whether anything becomes stronger by being removed.

Then ask the only question that ultimately matters:

**After this node, is the visitor more likely to keep exploring?**

If the answer is no:

**do not publish it.**
